What Is a Storyboard?

What is a storyboard? In a nutshell, it's like a comic book, with each shot drawn out in order. This is basically your movie's guide – it outlines every key moment, angle, and visual detail.


These visual panels help you get your vision across to your whole crew, from the cinematographer to the set designer. We've seen directors at our Louisiana production facility use all sorts of visuals. The magic's in how well they get their point across.

The Evolution of Storyboarding in Film

Walt Disney actually started using this technique back in the 1930s, but not just for animated films. He soon applied it to live-action productions too. Many famous directors, like Alfred Hitchcock and Steven Spielberg, have relied on detailed storyboards.

What is a storyboard in film production today? Storyboarding now includes digital tools, 3D pre-visualization, and even using virtual reality. To best serve filmmakers, a film production services company these days needs to be familiar with both old-school hand-drawn techniques and the latest digital tools.

We're always stressing the same five key elements that make a storyboard really work

  1. Each panel should clearly show what's happening. Think about where your characters are, what props are important, and the overall setting. Your storyboard should get the main points across..
  2. Decide if you want a wide shot, close-up, or medium shot, and mark it down. Use simple arrows or notes to show camera movements.
  3. Use arrows and motion lines to show characters or objects moving in the frame. This is especially important for action scenes or any scene with a lot of movement.
  4. Some panels might be quick cuts, while others last longer. Make notes about timing when it matters.
  5. Include key dialogue, sound effects, or other important production notes. These notes help department heads understand what they're hearing and feeling.

The Purpose nd Benefits of Storyboarding

As a film production services company, we've seen firsthand how storyboards can make a big difference. They can impact your project in many ways, from planning and budgeting to actually shooting the film. We want to share some of the benefits we've seen:

  • Pre-Production Planning: Storyboards are key to spotting potential challenges. Say you need a specific piece of gear for a particular shot - your storyboard will flag this during planning.
  • Budget Control: By planning ahead, you can avoid expensive mistakes. Having a clear storyboard can save productions a lot of money; we've seen cases where it's prevented costly reshoots.
  • Creative Communication: A storyboard acts as a common language that the entire crew can understand. This means the entire team must be looking at the same blueprint.
  • Efficiency on Set: Having a solid storyboard makes your shoot days a lot more efficient. You'll waste less time deciding what to film and have more time to focus on getting each shot just right.

What Is a Storyboard in Film Production

What is a storyboard in film production that is different from other storyboarding forms? In film, you have to think about things like:

  • Cinematic Language: Your storyboard is where your understanding of the impact of different shots comes together. For example, using a close-up during a really emotional moment packs a different punch than a wide shot.
  • Technical Requirements: When you create a storyboard, jot down the lighting you'll need and any other special effects.
  • Continuity Planning: Films are rarely shot in order, so your storyboard is crucial. It helps tie everything together across different days and locations.

Types of Storyboards for Different Production Needs

  • Traditional Hand-Drawn Storyboards: Any director can pick up a pencil and paper to start bringing their ideas to life. Hand-drawn storyboards often capture the raw energy and spontaneity of the creative process.
  • Digital Storyboards: Digital storyboards are created with programs like Storyboard Pro or Photoshop. They're easy to tweak and share with others, making them a great asset to use alongside other tools.
  • Photo Storyboards: Some filmmakers create storyboards with photos, either by restaging scenes with stand-ins or using reference images. This method is especially helpful for complicated scenes.learnt
  • Animatics: These are animated storyboards that help you get a feel for what your final product will be like, and they're really handy for action scenes.

The Storyboarding Process: From Concept to Creation

We've seen how the most effective storyboarding processes play out:

  • Script Analysis: Start by taking a close look at your script. Identify the key visual moments, emotional beats, and technical challenges. So, what is the primary use of a storyboard at this point? It's to turn your written words into visual ideas.
  • Shot Planning: Break down each scene into individual shots. What coverage do you need for each one? Think about how these shots will fit together to create the final sequence.
  • Visual Research: Check out similar scenes in other films for inspiration. Collecting these visuals helps you make more informed calls on how to set up your shots.
  • Rough Sketching: Start with rough thumbnail sketches to test out different ideas. Composition and storytelling clarity are what matter at this point.
  • Refinement and Detail: After nailing down your basic approach, make your storyboard panels more detailed. Add some notes about how the camera will move and the timing.
  • Team Review and Revision: Share your storyboards with the people in charge. This team effort can result in better solutions.

Common Storyboarding Mistakes to Avoid

As a film production services company, we've seen some common mistakes that can really hurt the effectiveness of storyboards:

  • Over-Complicating the Visuals: Your storyboard doesn't have to be a work of art. What matters most is that it gets the point across. Simple drawings that clearly show what's happening are better than fancy artwork.
  • Ignoring Technical Limitations: Make sure your storyboarded shots are realistic. That amazing crane shot might seem perfect, but it'll be a pricey issue.
  • Neglecting Continuity: Keep an eye on where your characters are standing, which way the light is coming from, and other details that will matter a lot when you edit.
  • Rushing the Process: Storyboarding is a process that can't be rushed. When you try to speed through it to get to production quicker, you're likely to encounter problems down the line.

Advanced Storyboarding Techniques

If you're looking to improve your storyboarding skills, here are some advanced techniques:

  • Multi-Panel Sequences: For complex action sequences, use multiple panels. This helps choreograph the sequence and ensures everything works together.
  • Layered Storyboards: Create separate layers for different elements. This setup works really well for sequences with a lot of VFX.
  • Interactive Storyboards: Digital storyboards can be interactive, with elements you can click to see extra details or different ways to approach a scene.
  • Collaborative Storyboarding: Use cloud-based tools that let team members contribute. This way leads to better solutions and gets the whole team on board.
